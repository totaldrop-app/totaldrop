Privacy policy · version 6 · 4 November 2026

We collect nothing about you.

TotalDrop has no accounts, no analytics, no advertising and no crash reporting. This page explains exactly what our servers can see while you transfer files, and how quickly it is gone.

Who we are

TotalDrop is a free, open-source project maintained by Ozan Özdil. It applies to the TotalDrop apps, the command-line client and the servers at totaldrop.app. Contact: privacy@totaldrop.app.

What the apps collect

Nothing. The apps do not ask for your name, email address or phone number, and they contain no tracking, analytics, advertising or crash-reporting code. Up to 0.3.0-beta.2 the Android app needs only internet access. Later versions ask only for what a transfer needs: internet access and network state, to connect; running a transfer in the background, so it continues while you switch to another app; and showing notifications, for the ongoing notification with its Stop button while a transfer runs. These permissions stay on your device: they send nothing to us or anyone else.

Your files

Files travel directly from one device to the other when the two can reach each other, and otherwise through our relay (below); either way they are encrypted end to end. Our servers never see your files, their names or their contents, and cannot read what passes through them: the keys exist only on the two devices.

What the rendezvous server sees

To start a transfer, both devices connect to our rendezvous server at rendezvous.totaldrop.app, which runs on Cloudflare. It helps two devices that share a code find each other. While it does so it necessarily sees:

  • the IP address of each device, as any internet server does;
  • the number at the start of the code (for example the 7 in 7-harbor-rocket), but never the words;
  • when you connect and the size of the short messages the two devices exchange. After the first two, these messages are encrypted so that only the other device can read them.

The server writes none of this to a log. A mailbox and everything in it is deleted as soon as both devices leave, and after one hour at the latest. After every transfer the code's number rests for 10 minutes before it is given out again; after a failed code attempt, for 24 hours.

Cloudflare, as our infrastructure provider, processes IP addresses to deliver and protect the service under its own privacy policy. The same applies to the relay. We have turned off request logs and Workers logging for both servers.

Finding a direct path

To connect across the internet, each device first asks a STUN server which public IP address and port it appears to have. By default this is Cloudflare's free STUN service at stun.cloudflare.com. It sees your IP address, as any internet server does, and nothing else. In privacy mode (below) the device does not ask it.

What the relay sees

Our relay at relay.totaldrop.app, which runs on Cloudflare, carries a transfer when the two devices cannot reach each other directly. In Android and Linux 0.2.0-beta.1 and later, and in the command line built from the repository since 29 October 2026, a device contacts the relay only if no direct connection has been chosen about 1.2 seconds after the two devices start connecting. It contacts the relay at once when either device is in privacy mode or the other device announces no address. A transfer whose direct connection is chosen in time reaches the relay only if that connection later goes quiet: in Android and Linux versions after 0.3.0-beta.3, and the command line that comes with them, a device that hears nothing from the other device over the direct connection for 8 seconds, because the network dropped or the other device paused, moves the transfer onto the relay. It goes back to the direct connection only if the other device is still sending over it; otherwise the transfer stays on the relay until it ends. This happens only when both devices use the same relay. If a direct connection is chosen later, the device closes its relay connection at that moment. Older versions (Android 0.1.0-beta.5, Linux 0.1.0-beta.4) connect to the relay at the start of every transfer, and a receiving device on those versions uses whichever relay the sender names; update to stop this. When the relay is used, it sees:

  • the IP address of each device;
  • two random keys made for this one transfer and never used again;
  • when each device connected and disconnected, and how much encrypted data passed.

The keys do not link one transfer to another, but your IP address can: the relay and the rendezvous server, both run by us on Cloudflare, see it moments apart. The relay cannot read the files, their names or anything about who you are. It writes nothing to a log and keeps nothing about the transfer once it ends.

Each device's relay address is shared with the other device, encrypted. If the sender uses a relay of its own (the command line's --relay option or TOTALDROP_RELAY), in 0.2.0-beta.1 and later the receiving device uses it only if it is also the receiver's own relay setting; otherwise it never contacts it. Whoever runs a relay that a device connects to sees what is listed above. When the two devices use different relays and have no direct path, the transfer stops with an error.

Privacy mode ("Hide my address from the other device" in the apps' Settings since 0.2.0-beta.1, --relay-only in the command line): your device announces no IP address to the other device, does not ask the STUN server, never dials the other device directly and refuses a direct connection even from the paired device. Every transfer goes through the relay, so the other device does not learn your IP address, whether you send or receive. It is slower, and the relay and the rendezvous server still see your IP address. The other device must use the same relay as you, whether you send or receive (the apps all use ours); otherwise the transfer stops with an error.

What the other device sees

To connect directly, the two devices exchange their network addresses with each other, encrypted. Each device therefore learns the other's local and public IP address, as with any direct connection. A device in privacy mode announces no address, so the other device does not learn it.

On your device

Received files are saved in Download/TotalDrop on Android and in ~/Downloads/TotalDrop on Linux. They are ordinary shared files, handled like your other downloads. When you send from Android, the files you pick are copied to the app's cache. The copies are deleted when you close a finished send or stop one. Copies of files you picked but did not send (you went back before sending, removed them from the list, or Android closed the app) can stay until a later send is closed or stopped, or until Android clears the cache. The apps keep two settings: the rendezvous server address and whether privacy mode is on (on Linux in ~/.config/totaldrop/settings.toml). The Android app's own data, these copies and settings, never goes into cloud backups or device-to-device transfers.

Children

TotalDrop is not directed at children and collects no personal data from anyone.

Your rights

Because we hold no personal data about you, there is nothing to access, correct or delete. If you have a question or a complaint, write to us at the address above. You also have the right to complain to your data protection authority.

Changes

Version 6 follows Android and Linux versions after 0.3.0-beta.3, in which a direct connection that goes quiet moves onto the relay, so the relay can see a transfer that started on a direct connection. Version 5 lists the permissions that Android versions after 0.3.0-beta.2 ask for, which let a transfer continue in the background with a notification; what our servers see does not change. Version 4 follows Android and Linux 0.2.0-beta.1, which changed what the relay and the other device see. In these versions devices contact the relay only when no direct connection is chosen in time or there is no direct path to try, instead of at the start of every transfer. A receiver no longer uses a relay that only the sender chose, so privacy mode now hides a receiver's address too. Privacy mode is now also in the apps' Settings and refuses direct connections. Version 4 also says what the Android app keeps in its cache. Version 3 corrected version 2, which said the apps reach the relay only when no direct path works (they then connected to it at the start of every transfer) and that privacy mode hid a receiver's address, which it then did not. It also named the Linux app's files. Version 2 added the STUN service and the relay. Features planned for later versions (links, optional anonymous accounts) will change what our servers handle. We will update this policy before any such feature ships, and keep every version in the project's public repository.
